# Theory and numerical methods

This document explains, from the ground up, the model that `hlmodel` solves and
how it solves it. It is written for a reader who is comfortable with calculus
(derivatives, integrals, Taylor expansions, simple ordinary differential
equations, complex exponentials) and with basic linear algebra (vectors,
matrices, eigenvalues, solving linear systems), but who is **not** assumed to
know numerical analysis. Every numerical idea — discretising a PDE, finite
differences, implicit vs. explicit time stepping, stiffness, banded solves,
root-finding, fixed-point iteration, Floquet multipliers, Newton's method — is
introduced where it is first needed.

Throughout we use the **reduced units** of Hébraud & Lequeux (HL):

$$\tau = \sigma_c = G_0 = 1.$$

Time is measured in units of the elementary restructuring time $\tau$, stress in
units of the local yield stress $\sigma_c$ of a mesoscopic block, and modulus in
units of the elastic shear modulus $G_0$. Setting them to one removes clutter;
to restore physical units, multiply each quantity by the appropriate
combination of $\tau, \sigma_c, G_0$ at the end.

---

## Part I — The physics

### 1. The model

HL picture a soft glassy material (a paste, an emulsion, a dense foam, a
concentrated suspension) as a large collection of **mesoscopic blocks**. Each
block is small enough to be roughly uniform but large enough to deform
elastically and occasionally yield. The state of a block is summarised by one
number: the local shear stress $\sigma$ it currently carries.

Instead of tracking every block, we track the **probability distribution**
$P(\sigma, t)$ — the fraction of blocks whose local stress lies near $\sigma$ at
time $t$. By definition it is normalised,

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} P(\sigma, t)\, d\sigma = 1,$$

and the experimentally measured **macroscopic stress** is its average (first
moment),

$$\Sigma(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \sigma\, P(\sigma, t)\, d\sigma.$$

Four physical processes change $P$ in time, and each contributes a term to its
evolution equation:

1. **Elastic loading.** When the material is sheared at rate $\dot\gamma$, every
   block's stress increases elastically at rate $\dot\gamma$ (in reduced units
   $G_0 = 1$, so $\dot\sigma = G_0\dot\gamma = \dot\gamma$). A population whose
   stress all drifts at the same speed is described by an **advection** (drift)
   term $-\dot\gamma\,\partial_\sigma P$: probability is carried bodily along the
   $\sigma$-axis.

2. **Mechanical noise.** When one block somewhere yields, it suddenly releases
   stress, and that release kicks the stresses of *other* blocks up or down a
   little. Summed over many random plastic events elsewhere, each block feels a
   fluctuating force — effectively a random walk in stress. A random walk is
   described by a **diffusion** term $D\,\partial^2_\sigma P$, where $D$ is the
   intensity of the noise. (Crucially $D$ is not externally imposed; it is
   generated by the yielding itself — see the closures below.)

3. **Plastic yielding.** A block whose stress exceeds the threshold,
   $|\sigma| > \sigma_c = 1$, becomes unstable and yields. HL take this to happen
   at a fixed rate $1/\tau = 1$. This **removes** probability from the
   over-threshold region: a loss term $-\,H(|\sigma| - 1)\,P$, where $H$ is the
   Heaviside step function, $H(x) = 1$ for $x > 0$ and $0$ otherwise. So blocks
   with $|\sigma| > 1$ disappear at rate $1$, and blocks with $|\sigma| \le 1$ are
   untouched. (At exactly $|\sigma| = 1$, $H(0) = 0$: the threshold itself does
   not yield. This boundary convention matters for the discretisation.)

4. **Reinjection.** A block that yields relaxes to (nearly) zero stress and
   starts over. So every block lost to yielding **reappears** at $\sigma = 0$.
   This is a source term concentrated at the origin, $+\,\Gamma\,\delta(\sigma)$,
   where $\delta$ is the Dirac delta and $\Gamma$ is the total rate at which
   blocks are yielding (and therefore being reinjected).

Putting the four terms together gives the **Hébraud–Lequeux equation**:

$$
\boxed{\;
\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}
= -\,\dot\gamma\,\frac{\partial P}{\partial \sigma}
\;+\; D\,\frac{\partial^2 P}{\partial \sigma^2}
\;-\; H(|\sigma|-1)\,P
\;+\; \Gamma\,\delta(\sigma).
\;}
$$

### 2. The self-consistent closures

Two of the quantities above are not free parameters: they are determined by $P$
itself, which is what makes the model nonlinear and interesting.

**The reinjection rate equals the yielding rate.** Every block that yields is
reinjected, so the source strength $\Gamma$ must equal the total rate of
yielding:

$$
\Gamma(t) = \int_{|\sigma| > 1} P(\sigma, t)\, d\sigma .
$$

**The noise is generated by the yielding.** HL argue that the diffusion
intensity $D$ is proportional to how much plastic activity is going on, i.e. to
$\Gamma$:

$$
D(t) = \alpha\,\Gamma(t).
$$

The dimensionless number $\alpha$ — the single control parameter of the model —
measures how strongly a plastic event agitates its neighbours. Large $\alpha$:
each yield stirs the pot vigorously, keeping the material fluid. Small $\alpha$:
yields barely disturb anyone, and the material can get stuck.

**Conservation.** Notice the model conserves probability automatically. Integrate
the evolution equation over all $\sigma$. The advection and diffusion terms are
total derivatives and integrate to zero (the tails vanish). The yielding term
integrates to $-\int_{|\sigma|>1} P = -\Gamma$, and the source integrates to
$+\Gamma$. They cancel: $\frac{d}{dt}\int P\, d\sigma = 0$. Whatever yields is
exactly reinjected. We will insist that the numerical scheme inherit this
property *exactly*, because a method that slowly leaks or creates probability is
untrustworthy over the long runs that oscillatory rheology demands.

### 3. Two phases and a critical point

Everything hinges on $\alpha$. There is a **critical coupling**

$$\alpha_c = \tfrac12$$

separating two regimes:

- **Liquid** ($\alpha > \alpha_c$). The quiescent ($\dot\gamma = 0$) material has
  a unique steady distribution with $D > 0$: the spontaneous noise keeps blocks
  hopping over the threshold even with no external shear, so the material slowly
  flows and has a finite viscosity.
- **Jammed solid** ($\alpha < \alpha_c$). The noise cannot sustain itself; the
  only quiescent steady states have $D = 0$, and any distribution supported
  entirely within $[-1, 1]$ sits there forever. This **degeneracy** — a whole
  family of frozen states rather than one — is the mathematical signature of a
  glass, and a yield stress emerges.

We now derive $\alpha_c$ and the precise relation between $\alpha$ and $D$ in the
quiescent state, because that relation is the package's main correctness check.

### 4. The quiescent identity, derived in full

Set $\dot\gamma = 0$ and look for a time-independent solution, $\partial_t P =
0$. The equation becomes

$$
0 = D\,P''(\sigma) - H(|\sigma| - 1)\,P(\sigma) + \Gamma\,\delta(\sigma),
$$

with $\Gamma = \int_{|\sigma|>1}P$, $D = \alpha\Gamma$, $\int P = 1$, and
$P \to 0$ as $|\sigma| \to \infty$. We solve this elementary boundary-value
problem by hand.

**Symmetry.** With no shear the equation is unchanged under $\sigma \to
-\sigma$, so the solution is even: $P(\sigma) = P(-\sigma)$. We may work on
$\sigma \ge 0$ and double integrals at the end.

**Solve region by region.** Away from $\sigma = 0$ (no source) and away from
$\sigma = 1$ (where $H$ switches), the equation is a constant-coefficient linear
ODE, different on each side of the threshold.

*Inside the threshold, $0 < \sigma < 1$* (here $H = 0$):

$$D\,P'' = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P'' = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
P(\sigma) = a - b\,\sigma,$$

a straight line. (We expect $P$ to decrease away from $\sigma = 0$, so $b > 0$.)
By evenness, near the origin $P(\sigma) = a - b\,|\sigma|$.

*Outside the threshold, $\sigma > 1$* (here $H = 1$):

$$D\,P'' - P = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P'' = \frac{P}{D}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(\sigma) = C\,e^{-\sigma/\sqrt D} + C'\,e^{+\sigma/\sqrt D}.$$

The growing exponential must vanish ($C' = 0$) so that $P \to 0$ at infinity. It
is convenient to write the surviving piece with its origin shifted to the
threshold,

$$P(\sigma) = c\,e^{-(\sigma - 1)/\sqrt D}, \qquad \sigma > 1,$$

so that $c = P(1^+)$ is the value just outside the threshold.

**Match at the threshold $\sigma = 1$.** There is no source and no delta at
$\sigma = 1$; the only thing that happens is that $H$ turns on. Since $P$ is
bounded, $P''$ is bounded, so $P$ and its first derivative $P'$ are both
continuous there.

- Continuity of $P$: $\;a - b = c.$
- Continuity of $P'$: the inside slope is $-b$; the outside slope is
  $P'(1^+) = -c/\sqrt D$. Hence $-b = -c/\sqrt D$, i.e. $\;c = b\sqrt D.$

Combining, $a - b = b\sqrt D$, so

$$a = b\,(1 + \sqrt D).$$

**The source condition at $\sigma = 0$.** Integrate the stationary equation
across the origin, from $-\varepsilon$ to $+\varepsilon$, and let $\varepsilon
\to 0$. The yielding term contributes nothing (it is bounded and $H = 0$ near
$0$). The diffusion term gives $D\,[P'(0^+) - P'(0^-)]$, the jump in slope. The
delta gives $\Gamma$. So

$$D\,\big[P'(0^+) - P'(0^-)\big] + \Gamma = 0.$$

Near the origin $P = a - b|\sigma|$, so $P'(0^+) = -b$ and $P'(0^-) = +b$; the
slope jumps by $-2b$. Therefore

$$\Gamma = 2bD.$$

(The Dirac source injects probability at the origin, which is carried away to
either side by diffusion — hence the kink, a discontinuity in slope, at
$\sigma = 0$.)

**Normalisation.** The total probability is one. Using evenness,

$$
1 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} P\, d\sigma = 2\int_0^{\infty} P\, d\sigma
= 2\left[\int_0^1 (a - b\sigma)\, d\sigma + \int_1^{\infty} c\,e^{-(\sigma-1)/\sqrt D}\, d\sigma\right].
$$

The first integral is $a - b/2$; the second is $c\sqrt D$. Substituting
$c = b\sqrt D$ (so $c\sqrt D = bD$) and then $a = b(1 + \sqrt D)$,

$$
\int_0^\infty P = a - \tfrac{b}{2} + bD
= b\left(1 + \sqrt D - \tfrac12 + D\right)
= b\left(\tfrac12 + \sqrt D + D\right),
$$

so normalisation reads

$$2b\left(\tfrac12 + \sqrt D + D\right) = 1.$$

**Close the loop.** Finally impose the noise closure $D = \alpha\Gamma$ with
$\Gamma = 2bD$:

$$D = \alpha\,(2bD) \;\Longrightarrow\; 1 = 2\alpha b \;\Longrightarrow\;
b = \frac{1}{2\alpha} \qquad (D \neq 0).$$

Set this equal to the value of $b$ from normalisation,
$b = \big[\,2(\tfrac12 + \sqrt D + D)\,\big]^{-1}$, and the $2$'s cancel:

$$
\boxed{\;\alpha = \tfrac12 + \sqrt D + D\;}
$$

This is the function `alpha_of_D_quiescent(D)` in the code; its inverse (solve
the quadratic in $x = \sqrt D$) is `D_quiescent(alpha)`.

**Consistency check.** As a sanity check the yielding integral must reproduce
the jump value of $\Gamma$:
$\int_{|\sigma|>1}P = 2\int_1^\infty c\,e^{-(\sigma-1)/\sqrt D}\,d\sigma
= 2c\sqrt D = 2(b\sqrt D)\sqrt D = 2bD$, which indeed equals $\Gamma$. Good — as
promised, "reinjection = yielding" holds automatically.

**What it tells us.**

- Setting $D = 0$ gives $\alpha = \tfrac12$: the critical coupling is
  $\alpha_c = \tfrac12$, the boundary below which no self-sustaining noise (hence
  no spontaneous flow) is possible.
- For $\alpha$ just above $\alpha_c$, $D$ is small and $\sqrt D$ dominates $D$,
  so $\alpha - \tfrac12 \approx \sqrt D$, giving

$$D \sim (\alpha - \alpha_c)^2 \qquad (\alpha \to \alpha_c^+).$$

The diffusion (and the inverse relaxation time it sets) switches on quadratically
as we cross into the liquid.

The discrete steady solver reproduces $\alpha = \tfrac12 + \sqrt D + D$ to machine
precision (see `tests/test_steady.py`). Because that single number depends on the
operators, the boundary treatment, the integration rule, *and* the
self-consistency loop all being right, it is a stringent end-to-end test.

### 5. Other results the package reproduces

These are checked numerically rather than derived here, but it helps to know what
each one means.

**Flow curves.** The steady macroscopic stress as a function of shear rate,
$\Sigma(\dot\gamma)$, behaves differently in the two phases:

- *At the critical point* $\alpha = \alpha_c$, the stress vanishes as a fractional
  power of the rate,
  $$\Sigma \sim \dot\gamma^{\,1/5} \qquad (\dot\gamma \to 0).$$
  The exponent $1/5$ is a hallmark of the HL model.
- *In the jammed phase* $\alpha < \alpha_c$, the material does not flow until the
  stress exceeds a finite **dynamic yield stress** $\sigma_y$. The flow curve has
  the **Herschel–Bulkley** form $\Sigma = \sigma_y + A\,\dot\gamma^{\,n}$, and the
  yield stress grows as one moves deeper into the solid,
  $$\sigma_y \sim (\alpha_c - \alpha)^{1/2}.$$

(The $1/5$ critical exponent and the rate exponent $n$ on the solid branch are
different quantities; do not conflate them.) The package's measured exponents are
$\approx 0.20$ and $\approx 0.51$.

**Linear viscoelasticity.** Probe the liquid with a tiny oscillatory strain of
frequency $\omega$ and measure the in-phase (elastic, "storage") modulus
$G'(\omega)$ and the out-of-phase (viscous, "loss") modulus $G''(\omega)$. At low
frequency the response is **Maxwellian** — like a spring and dashpot in series —

$$G'(\omega) \sim \omega^2, \qquad G''(\omega) \sim \omega \quad (\omega \to 0),$$

with a single relaxation time $\tau_{\rm relax}$ that diverges as $\alpha \to
\alpha_c^+$ (the liquid gets more and more sluggish near the glass transition). At
high frequency the material has no time to flow and responds elastically,
$G' \to G_0 = 1$. The SAOS solver reproduces all of this (`tests/test_saos_laos.py`).

## Part II — Turning the PDE into something a computer can solve

### 6. Discretisation: from a function to a vector

A computer cannot store the value of $P(\sigma, t)$ at every one of the infinitely
many points $\sigma$. The first step in any numerical PDE method is
**discretisation**: replace the continuous variable by a finite grid of sample
points and the continuous function by the list of its values there.

**The grid** (`grid.py`). We cover the interval $[-\sigma_{\max},
\sigma_{\max}]$ with $n$ equally spaced nodes
$\sigma_0, \sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_{n-1}$, separated by $h$. We deliberately
choose the spacing $h = 1/n_{\text{per\_unit}}$ so that the three special points
$\sigma = 0$ and $\sigma = \pm 1$ land *exactly* on nodes. This keeps the Dirac
source pinned to a node and the yielding switch aligned with the mesh, both of
which keep the bookkeeping exact. The distribution becomes a vector
$P = (P_0, P_1, \dots, P_{n-1})$ with $P_i \approx P(\sigma_i, t)$.

Integrals become sums. We use the simplest rule, the **rectangle rule**,

$$\int f(\sigma)\, d\sigma \;\approx\; h\sum_i f(\sigma_i).$$

A fancier rule (trapezoid, Simpson) is slightly more accurate for smooth
integrands, but the rectangle rule has a property we value more here: it makes the
discrete statement "yielding removes exactly what the source reinjects" hold
*identically*, so the scheme conserves probability to machine precision.

**Approximating derivatives by finite differences.** Taylor's theorem turns
derivatives into differences of nearby values. Expanding $P(\sigma \pm h)$ about
$\sigma$,

$$P(\sigma \pm h) = P(\sigma) \pm h P'(\sigma) + \tfrac{h^2}{2}P''(\sigma)
\pm \tfrac{h^3}{6}P'''(\sigma) + \cdots,$$

and combining the two:

$$
P'(\sigma) \approx \frac{P(\sigma + h) - P(\sigma - h)}{2h}
\quad\text{(centered first difference)},
$$
$$
P''(\sigma) \approx \frac{P(\sigma + h) - 2P(\sigma) + P(\sigma - h)}{h^2}
\quad\text{(centered second difference)}.
$$

Each is exact in the limit $h \to 0$; for finite $h$ the leading error is
$O(h^2)$. In vector form, "take the second difference at every node" is a linear
operation — multiplication by a matrix. For instance the second-difference
operator $L_2$ has $-2/h^2$ on its diagonal and $+1/h^2$ on the two neighbouring
diagonals; multiplying the vector $P$ by $L_2$ produces the vector of approximate
second derivatives.

**The PDE becomes a system of ODEs.** Replacing each spatial operator in the HL
equation by its matrix turns the single partial differential equation into a
large system of *ordinary* differential equations, one per node:

$$\frac{dP}{dt} = G\,P, \qquad G = D\,L_2 + A + \text{Yield} + \text{Source},$$

where $A$ is the advection matrix, $\text{Yield}$ the diagonal yielding matrix,
and $\text{Source}$ the reinjection matrix (all built in `operators.py`, and all
detailed below). The matrix $G$ is the **generator** of the dynamics. Almost
everything the package does is built on this one object: stationary states are
its null vectors, time stepping integrates $dP/dt = GP$, and linear response uses
its eigen-structure.

### 7. Conservation as a property of the matrix

Because conservation matters so much, it pays to see exactly what it means for the
matrix $G$. The total probability is the sum $M = h\sum_i P_i$. Its rate of
change is

$$
\frac{dM}{dt} = h\sum_i \frac{dP_i}{dt} = h\sum_i (G P)_i
= h\sum_i \sum_j G_{ij} P_j = h\sum_j \Big(\sum_i G_{ij}\Big) P_j .
$$

For this to vanish for *every* possible state $P$, we need the bracket to vanish
for each $j$:

$$\sum_i G_{ij} = 0 \quad\text{for every column } j.$$

So **"every column of $G$ sums to zero" is precisely the discrete statement of
conservation of probability.** Equivalently, the row vector of all ones is a left
null vector of $G$: $\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T} G = 0$. We will build the operators so
that this holds exactly (not just approximately), which is what guarantees no
probability leaks away over long runs.

### 8. The operators, in conservative (flux) form

A naive finite-difference discretisation does not automatically have zero column
sums, especially at the boundaries. To guarantee conservation we use the
**finite-volume** viewpoint, which builds it in by construction.

**Bins and fluxes.** Think of each node $\sigma_i$ as the centre of a little bin
of width $h$, and think of $P_i$ as the amount of probability in that bin.
Probability moves between adjacent bins by crossing the **face** between them.
Let $J_{i+1/2}$ denote the flux (rate of flow) through the face between bin $i$
and bin $i+1$. Then the amount in bin $i$ changes only because of what crosses its
two faces:

$$\frac{dP_i}{dt} = -\,\frac{J_{i+1/2} - J_{i-1/2}}{h} \;+\; (\text{reaction})_i .$$

Here is the magic: whatever leaves bin $i$ through its right face is *exactly*
what enters bin $i+1$ through its left face, because both use the same number
$J_{i+1/2}$. When we add up $dP_i/dt$ over all bins, the flux terms **telescope** —
each interior face appears once with a $+$ and once with a $-$ and cancels —
leaving only the two outermost faces:

$$\sum_i \big(J_{i+1/2} - J_{i-1/2}\big) = J_{\text{right end}} - J_{\text{left end}}.$$

If we forbid probability from leaving the domain (a **no-flux** boundary,
$J = 0$ at the two outer faces), this is zero, and the advection + diffusion part
conserves mass exactly. That is the structural reason the package's generator has
exactly zero column sums.

The physical flux has two pieces, $J = \dot\gamma\,P - D\,\partial_\sigma P$: a
drift piece (advection) carrying probability along at speed $\dot\gamma$, and a
diffusive piece flowing down the gradient.

**Diffusion: the reflecting Laplacian.** Discretising the diffusive flux
$-D\,\partial_\sigma P$ on the faces and differencing gives, at interior nodes, the
familiar second difference $D\,(P_{i+1} - 2P_i + P_{i-1})/h^2$. At the two end
nodes the no-flux condition removes the outward term, leaving $D\,(P_1 - P_0)/h^2$
on the left and $D\,(P_{n-2} - P_{n-1})/h^2$ on the right (a "$-1$" instead of
"$-2$" on the boundary diagonal). This is the **reflecting** (Neumann) Laplacian;
its rows — and therefore its columns — sum to zero, as conservation requires.

**Advection: upwinding, and why.** Advection is pure transport: a shape slides
along the $\sigma$-axis at speed $\dot\gamma$ without changing. To compute the
advective flux $\dot\gamma\,P$ at a face we need the value of $P$ *at the face*,
but $P$ is stored at bin centres. Which neighbour should we use? The physically
correct choice is the **upwind** one — the bin the flow is coming *from*:

$$
J^{\text{adv}}_{i+1/2} =
\begin{cases}
\dot\gamma\,P_i & \dot\gamma > 0 \ (\text{flow to the right, take left bin}),\\[2pt]
\dot\gamma\,P_{i+1} & \dot\gamma < 0 \ (\text{flow to the left, take right bin}).
\end{cases}
$$

Why not just average the two neighbours (a centered scheme)? Because a centered
advection scheme has no sense of direction and tends to produce spurious
wiggles — over- and under-shoots — near sharp features, and it can even make $P$
go negative. This matters here: in the jammed phase $D \to 0$, the equation
becomes advection-dominated, and the distribution develops sharp fronts as it is
pushed against the threshold at $\pm 1$. Upwinding stays **monotone** (no spurious
oscillations) there. The price is a small amount of artificial smearing, called
**numerical diffusion**, which behaves like an extra diffusion of size
$\sim |\dot\gamma| h / 2$. We keep it under control by refining the grid (smaller
$h$) and by cross-checking against the analytic steady and SAOS solvers, which
have no such error. The code's advection matrix is sign-aware (so it works when
$\dot\gamma$ changes sign during an oscillation) and uses no-flux ends, preserving
conservation.

**Yielding.** This term is local — each over-threshold bin loses probability at
rate $1$, independently of its neighbours — so it is a diagonal matrix: entry
$-1$ on nodes with $|\sigma_i| > 1$, and $0$ elsewhere (including exactly at
$\pm 1$, per the $H(0) = 0$ convention).

**Source.** Reinjection deposits all the yielded probability at $\sigma = 0$. The
Dirac delta $\delta(\sigma)$ on the grid becomes a spike of height $1/h$ at the
zero node (so that $h \times 1/h = 1$, i.e. its discrete integral is one). Its
strength is the yielding rate $\Gamma = h\sum_{|\sigma_i|>1} P_i$. The result is a
matrix with a single non-trivial row (the zero-node row), which reads off the
total over-threshold probability and dumps it at the origin. Conveniently the
factor $h$ from the integral and the $1/h$ from the discrete delta cancel, so the
entries are just $1$. Together, the yielding loss and the source gain have
opposite, cancelling column sums — so the "reaction" part conserves mass on its
own, separately from the flux part.

The full generator $G = D\,L_2 + A + \text{Yield} + \text{Source}$ therefore has
exactly zero column sums: probability is conserved to machine precision, by
construction. (The test `test_generator_is_conservative` verifies this directly.)

## Part III — The solvers

### 9. Steady shear: a direct "oracle" and a time-stepping "engine"

For steady shear we want the stationary distribution at a fixed rate $\dot\gamma$
and coupling $\alpha$. The package computes it two independent ways and checks
they agree to about eleven digits — a strong guard against bugs, since two
different methods are unlikely to be wrong in the same way.

#### 9.1 The direct stationary solver

A stationary state satisfies $G\,P = 0$. There is a subtlety: because $G$
conserves mass, the constant row vector $\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}$ is a left null
vector, which forces $G$ to be **singular** (non-invertible). Concretely, the
equation $G P = 0$ does not have a single solution — it has a whole one-parameter
family: if $P$ is a solution, so is any multiple $cP$. (This just reflects the
fact that the *shape* of the stationary distribution is fixed but its overall
scale is set by a separate condition.) In linear-algebra language, $G$ has
**rank** $n - 1$: it pins down all of $P$ except one overall degree of freedom.

We remove that ambiguity with the physical normalisation $\int P = 1$. Numerically
we **replace one (now-redundant) row** of the system $GP = 0$ with the equation
$h\sum_i P_i = 1$, and solve the resulting non-singular linear system for the
unique normalised stationary distribution. (Replacing a row is legitimate because,
by conservation, the rows of $G$ are linearly dependent — one of them carries no
new information — so we lose nothing by swapping it for the normalisation.) This is
`stationary_distribution(D, gammadot, grid)`.

But that gave us $P$ for a *given* $D$, whereas the physical $D$ must satisfy the
closure $D = \alpha\,\Gamma$. So we have one scalar equation left to solve for one
unknown $D$. Define the **residual**

$$r(D) = \alpha\,\Gamma\big(P(D)\big) - D,$$

the mismatch between the noise the closure demands and the noise we assumed. A
self-consistent state is a **root** $r(D) = 0$. Since $r$ is positive at small $D$
(a little shear pushes some probability over the threshold, so $\alpha\Gamma > D$)
and negative at large $D$ (strong diffusion spreads $P$ out but $D$ has outrun
it), $r$ changes sign exactly once, and we locate the crossing by a standard,
robust **root-finder** (bracket the sign change on a coarse scan, then squeeze it
with Brent's method, a safeguarded bisection). This is `solve_steady`. The whole
calculation involves no time and no relaxation, which is exactly why it is fast and
accurate even where the time-stepping engine would crawl.

*One honest limitation.* Very close to $\alpha_c$, the physical $D$ is tiny; the
stationary solve becomes ill-conditioned as $D \to 0$ (the diffusion that couples
the grid points is too weak to dominate the discretisation), and the result
becomes resolution-limited. The fix is simply a finer grid; the code floors the
search at $D \sim 4h^2$, below which a finer mesh is needed. (This is what tripped
an early version of the yield-stress test: at too small a rate the solver returned
the floored value. Extracting $\sigma_y$ by fitting the Herschel–Bulkley form at
resolvable rates and extrapolating sidesteps it.)

#### 9.2 The transient engine

The second route simply integrates $dP/dt = GP$ forward in time until it stops
changing. It is the subject of the next section, and it doubles as the inner
machinery of the LAOS solver.

### 10. Time stepping: explicit, implicit, stiffness, and IMEX

To follow $P$ in time we step from $P^n$ (at time $t_n$) to $P^{n+1}$ (at
$t_{n+1} = t_n + \Delta t$). The art is choosing *how* to evaluate the right-hand
side $GP$ during the step.

**Explicit vs. implicit.** The simplest choice, **explicit (forward) Euler**,
evaluates the right-hand side at the old, known state:
$P^{n+1} = P^n + \Delta t\,G\,P^n$. Cheap — just a matrix–vector product — but it
can be unstable unless $\Delta t$ is small. The opposite choice, **implicit
(backward) Euler**, evaluates it at the new, unknown state:
$P^{n+1} = P^n + \Delta t\,G\,P^{n+1}$, which rearranges to the linear system
$(I - \Delta t\,G)\,P^{n+1} = P^n$. More work — we must *solve* a linear system
each step — but far more stable.

**Why diffusion forces tiny explicit steps ("stiffness").** Consider a single
decaying mode $u' = -\lambda u$ with $\lambda > 0$. Explicit Euler gives
$u_{n+1} = (1 - \lambda\Delta t)\,u_n$, which stays bounded only if
$|1 - \lambda\Delta t| \le 1$, i.e. $\Delta t \le 2/\lambda$. The trouble is that
the discrete diffusion operator $D\,L_2$ has modes as fast as $\lambda \approx
4D/h^2$ (the most rapidly oscillating grid mode). Plugging in, explicit stability
demands

$$\Delta t \;\lesssim\; \frac{h^2}{2D}.$$

As we refine the grid ($h \to 0$) this becomes punishingly small — halving $h$
quarters the allowed step. A problem with such a fast mode dictating a tiny step,
even when we only care about slow behaviour, is called **stiff**. Implicit Euler
on the same mode gives $u_{n+1} = u_n/(1 + \lambda\Delta t)$, which is stable for
*any* $\Delta t$ — so we treat the stiff diffusion implicitly and escape the
restriction.

**IMEX: implicit–explicit splitting.** Not every term needs implicit treatment.
The package uses an **IMEX** scheme: treat the parts that are stiff or stability-
limiting (diffusion *and* advection) implicitly, and the cheap, bounded parts
(yielding and source) explicitly. The update is

$$
\big(I - \theta\,\Delta t\,(D\,L_2 + A)\big)\,P^{n+1}
= P^n + \Delta t\,(\text{Yield} + \text{Source})\,P^n + (\text{old-level terms}),
$$

where the parameter $\theta$ selects backward Euler ($\theta = 1$, maximally
robust) or the **Crank–Nicolson** scheme ($\theta = \tfrac12$, which averages old
and new levels and is more accurate).

**Why this particular split conserves mass exactly.** Recall that mass is
conserved iff the operator applied each step has zero column sums. The implicit
operator $D\,L_2 + A$ has zero column sums on its own. The explicit pair
$\text{Yield} + \text{Source}$ *also* has zero column sums on its own (the loss and
the gain cancel) — *provided both are evaluated at the same time level*, which is
why we keep them together at the old level rather than splitting one implicit and
one explicit. With both halves individually conservative, total probability is
preserved to machine precision every step, for any $\Delta t$. (Verified in
`test_mass_conserved_*`.)

**Banded solves keep each step cheap.** The implicit matrix
$M = I - \theta\Delta t(D L_2 + A)$ is **tridiagonal**: it has non-zeros only on
the main diagonal and the two adjacent diagonals, because each grid point couples
only to its immediate neighbours. Solving a tridiagonal system $Mx = b$ takes work
proportional to $n$ (the Thomas algorithm — Gaussian elimination specialised to
three diagonals), as opposed to the $\sim n^3$ cost of a general matrix. The code
assembles the three diagonals as plain `numpy` arrays — no loops, no sparse-matrix
overhead — and calls a banded solver, so each time step is fast.

**The lagged nonlinearity, and why the scheme is first order in time.** There is
one genuine nonlinearity: $D = \alpha\Gamma(P)$ depends on $P$ itself. We handle
it by **lagging** — using $D$ computed from the old state $P^n$ inside the step.
This keeps the linear-algebra linear. The cost is accuracy in time: even with
Crank–Nicolson ($\theta = \tfrac12$) on the linear part, the lagged $D$ and the
old-level reaction terms make the overall scheme **first order in $\Delta t$**
(error $\propto \Delta t$). An optional **Picard sub-iteration** — recompute $D$
from the freshly updated $P$ and redo the step a couple of times — tightens the
closure within a step and helps near criticality.

*A word on "order of accuracy."* A time-stepping scheme is called **$p$-th order**
if its error shrinks like $\Delta t^{\,p}$ as $\Delta t \to 0$. A practical
consequence: halving $\Delta t$ should cut the error by $2^p$. So if you halve
$\Delta t$ and the error halves (ratio $2$), the scheme is first order ($p = 1$);
if the error quarters (ratio $4$), it is second order. We use exactly this test to
validate the SAOS↔LAOS limit below: the discrepancy halved each time we halved
$\Delta t$, confirming the first-order behaviour and that it extrapolates to zero.
For high-precision *linear* response, don't fight the $\Delta t$ error at all — use
the SAOS solver, which is exact in time.

### 11. SAOS: linear response by perturbation theory

Small-amplitude oscillatory shear (SAOS) applies a tiny sinusoidal strain and
measures the linear moduli. Because the amplitude is infinitesimal, we do not need
to simulate anything in time — we **linearise** the dynamics around the quiescent
state and solve one linear problem per frequency. This works only in the liquid
phase ($\alpha > \alpha_c$), where the quiescent state is unique.

**Perturbation expansion.** Let $P_0(\sigma)$ be the quiescent base state (with
noise $D_0$), the solution of §9.1 at $\dot\gamma = 0$. Drive the system with a
small strain $\gamma(t) = \gamma_0 \sin\omega t$, and write the response as the
base state plus a small correction proportional to $\gamma_0$:

$$P(\sigma, t) = P_0(\sigma) + \gamma_0\,\mathrm{Re}\!\big[p_1(\sigma)\,e^{i\omega t}\big] + O(\gamma_0^2).$$

Here we have used the standard trick of representing a sinusoid as the real part
of a complex exponential $e^{i\omega t}$; the complex amplitude $p_1(\sigma)$
carries both the size and the phase lag of the response at each stress. Substitute
into the HL equation and keep only terms linear in $\gamma_0$. The time derivative
of $e^{i\omega t}$ brings down a factor $i\omega$, which turns the time-dependent
PDE into a purely **algebraic** (in time) linear system for $p_1$:

$$
\big(i\omega\,I - G_{\rm lin}\big)\,p_1 = -\,\omega\,\partial_\sigma P_0,
$$

where the right-hand side is the forcing from shearing the base state, and
$G_{\rm lin}$ is the generator linearised about $P_0$:

$$
G_{\rm lin} = D_0 L_2 + \text{Yield} + \text{Source}
\;+\; \alpha\,(L_2 P_0)\,(h\,\text{mask})^{\mathsf T}.
$$

The first three terms are the familiar operators evaluated at the base noise
$D_0$. The last term is new: it is the **feedback of the noise**. When the
oscillation changes the yielding rate by a little, the closure $D = \alpha\Gamma$
changes the diffusion by a little ($D_1 = \alpha\Gamma_1$), which in turn pushes
the base state around through $D_1\,L_2 P_0$. This makes the problem **nonlocal**:
the change in diffusion depends on an integral of $p_1$ over the whole
over-threshold region (that is what the $(\text{mask})^{\mathsf T}$ encodes).

**A structural gift: rank-one update + Sherman–Morrison.** A nonlocal term would
normally destroy the cheap tridiagonal structure, because it couples every node to
every over-threshold node — turning the matrix dense, with $\sim n^3$ solve cost.
But notice that *two* of the terms — the reinjection source and the noise-feedback
term — both act through the same "row," proportional to $\text{mask}^{\mathsf T}$
(both respond to the same integral $\sum_{|\sigma|>1} p_1$). Their sum is therefore
a single **rank-one** matrix: an outer product $u\,v^{\mathsf T}$ of one column
vector with one row vector. So the full matrix is

$$\underbrace{i\omega I - (D_0 L_2 + \text{Yield})}_{\text{tridiagonal } T}
\;-\; u\,v^{\mathsf T},$$

a tridiagonal matrix plus a single rank-one correction. The **Sherman–Morrison
formula** inverts exactly this kind of object cheaply. It states that

$$(T - u v^{\mathsf T})^{-1} b = T^{-1}b + \frac{T^{-1}u\,(v^{\mathsf T} T^{-1}b)}{1 - v^{\mathsf T} T^{-1}u},$$

so we only ever solve two tridiagonal systems ($T x = b$ and $T y = u$) and then
combine the results with a couple of scalar dot products. (Intuitively, it is the
matrix version of the algebra you would do by hand to undo a single rank-one
change.) Each frequency therefore costs two fast banded solves — no dense linear
algebra anywhere. This is in `saos.py`.

**Reading off the moduli.** Having solved for $p_1$, the complex stress amplitude
is the first moment $S^\ast = h\sum_i \sigma_i\,(p_1)_i$. With our strain
convention $\gamma = \gamma_0\sin\omega t$, the macroscopic stress is
$\Sigma(t) = \gamma_0\big[G'\sin\omega t + G''\cos\omega t\big]$ to first order;
matching this against $\gamma_0\,\mathrm{Re}[S^\ast e^{i\omega t}]$ gives

$$G'(\omega) = -\,\mathrm{Im}\,S^\ast, \qquad G''(\omega) = \mathrm{Re}\,S^\ast.$$

At low frequency these reproduce the Maxwell laws $G' \sim \omega^2$, $G'' \sim
\omega$ (with $G'' > 0$, as a dissipative response must be); at high frequency
$G' \to G_0 = 1$. From the two low-frequency constants one can read off the
plateau modulus $G_0$ and the relaxation time $\tau_{\rm relax}$, a useful
cross-check (the `saos_spectrum.py` example does this and recovers $G_0 \approx
1$).

### 12. LAOS: the periodic response as a fixed point

At large amplitude the response is no longer a simple sinusoid; it contains higher
harmonics, and we must solve the full nonlinear dynamics. The key idea (this is
the heart of the package) is to recognise *what "steady state" becomes under a
periodic drive*.

**No fixed point, but a limit cycle.** With a periodic strain
$\gamma(t) = \gamma_0\sin\omega t$ there is no time-independent state. But after
initial transients die away, the system settles onto a **limit cycle** — a
response that repeats every period $T = 2\pi/\omega$:
$P(\sigma, t) = P(\sigma, t + T)$.

**The period map.** Define an operation $\mathcal M$ that takes a state at the
start of a period and returns the state one full period later, obtained by
integrating the dynamics from $t = 0$ to $t = T$:

$$\mathcal M : P(\cdot, 0) \;\longmapsto\; P(\cdot, T).$$

(This is also called the stroboscopic or Poincaré map: it photographs the system
once per period.) A response that repeats is one that comes back to where it
started after a period — a **fixed point** of $\mathcal M$:

$$\mathcal M(P_0) = P_0, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad F(P_0) \equiv \mathcal M(P_0) - P_0 = 0.$$

So we have converted "find the stationary state" into "find the fixed point of the
period map" — the same conceptual move, generalised to a forced problem. Because
the drive fixes both the period (it is $2\pi/\omega$, not unknown) and the phase,
there is no leftover freedom to pin down; it is a plain fixed-point equation.
Evaluating $\mathcal M$ once means integrating the transient solver of §10 over one
period.

**Solver 1: Picard iteration, and critical slowing down.** The most obvious way to
find the fixed point is to just keep applying the map — simulate period after
period and watch it converge: $P \leftarrow \mathcal M(P)$. How fast does the
leftover transient shrink? Linearise the map about the cycle: a small deviation
$\delta$ evolves over one period to $(\mathrm D\mathcal M)\,\delta$, where the
matrix $\mathrm D\mathcal M$ is called the **monodromy matrix**. Its eigenvalues
$\mu_k$ are the **Floquet multipliers** — they tell you the factor by which each
mode of a perturbation grows or shrinks per period. After $m$ periods the deviation
is multiplied by $\mu_{\max}^{\,m}$ (the largest multiplier dominates). So Picard
converges geometrically at rate $|\mu_{\max}|$ — fine when $|\mu_{\max}|$ is small,
but agonisingly slow when $|\mu_{\max}| \to 1$. And $|\mu_{\max}| \to 1$ is exactly
the interesting regime: small amplitude or $\alpha \to \alpha_c$, where the
material relaxes slowly. This is **critical slowing down**. (Starting Picard from
the quiescent base state — the package's default in the liquid phase — helps a lot
there, since the small-amplitude cycle is a small perturbation of it.)

**Solver 2: Newton, which beats the slowdown.** A better way to solve
$F(P_0) = 0$ is **Newton's method**. Recall the one-dimensional version: to solve
$f(x) = 0$, iterate $x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$ — follow the tangent line to
its root. It converges *quadratically* (the number of correct digits roughly
doubles each step) near a simple root, regardless of how flat or steep $f$ is. The
multivariable version replaces the derivative by the **Jacobian** matrix
$J = \partial F_i/\partial (P_0)_j$ and solves the linear system $J\,\delta = -F$
for the update $\delta$. Here $J = \mathrm D\mathcal M - I$, built from the same
monodromy matrix — so its convergence does *not* care whether $|\mu_{\max}|$ is
near $1$. Newton cures the critical slowing down.

**Jacobian-free Newton–Krylov.** There is one obstacle: the Jacobian $J$ is an
$n \times n$ matrix that we never want to form explicitly (it would require $n$
separate period integrations to build, and $n$ is in the thousands). The escape is
that the linear system $J\delta = -F$ can be solved by an **iterative** method (a
Krylov-subspace method such as GMRES) that needs only to compute *products* $J v$
for given vectors $v$ — never the matrix itself. And a product $J v$ is just a
directional derivative, which we approximate by a finite difference of the map:

$$J v \approx \frac{\mathcal M(P_0 + \varepsilon v) - \mathcal M(P_0)}{\varepsilon}
\;-\; v,$$

i.e. one extra one-period integration per product. Combining Newton's method with a
Krylov solver that only needs these products is **Jacobian-free Newton–Krylov**
(`scipy.optimize.newton_krylov`), the package's default LAOS solver. It is robust
across all regimes; Picard-from-base is offered as a simple, fast alternative in
the liquid phase.

**One gauge to fix.** Because $\mathcal M$ conserves mass, the residual $F$ always
sums to zero, and the Jacobian is singular along the constant direction (the
conserved-mass mode, with multiplier exactly $1$). This is harmless — we simply
renormalise the solution to unit mass, which fixes that one direction.

**Alignment with SAOS (the shared convention).** The whole package uses one strain
convention — $\gamma(t) = \gamma_0\sin\omega t$ with $t = 0$ pinned at $\gamma = 0$
— so that the SAOS and LAOS moduli mean the same thing. Once the cycle has
converged, we integrate one more period, sample the macroscopic stress
$\Sigma(t)$, and project it onto harmonics of the sine drive:

$$\Sigma(t) = \gamma_0\sum_{n}\big[G_n'\,\sin(n\omega t) + G_n''\,\cos(n\omega t)\big]$$

(this projection is `observables.decompose_oscillatory`). The first-harmonic
coefficients $G_1', G_1''$ are defined *identically* to the SAOS $G', G''$ — so as
the amplitude $\gamma_0 \to 0$ they must converge to the SAOS values. This is not an
assumption but a **test**: the package checks $G_1'(\gamma_0 \to 0) \to G'(\omega)$,
and confirms that the small residual at finite $\Delta t$ is just the time
integrator's first-order error (it halves when $\Delta t$ halves; see §10). This is
the practical answer to a real subtlety: since the *response* amplitude in LAOS is
not known ahead of time, one must solve the cycle self-consistently and then
extract the moduli with a phase convention that lines up with the linear theory —
otherwise the two would not be comparable.

**A free error diagnostic from symmetry.** With a sine drive the HL equation is
invariant under $(\sigma, t) \to (-\sigma,\, t + T/2)$. This symmetry forces the
stress to contain **odd harmonics only**. So the amplitude of the *even* harmonics,
which should be zero, is a free measure of numerical error — the package reports
it, and it sits at $\sim 10^{-8}$.

**The Floquet multiplier as a physical observable.** The dominant multiplier
$|\mu_{\max}|$ is not only a convergence certificate ($|\mu_{\max}| < 1$ means the
cycle is stable). It is also a physical quantity: it is essentially $\omega_c$, the
amplitude-dependent relaxation rate that HL plot — the rate at which the material
forgets a perturbation under large oscillatory shear. The package estimates it by
**power iteration** on the linearised map (repeatedly apply $\mathrm D\mathcal M$ to
a random vector and watch which direction it stretches; the stretch factor is
$|\mu_{\max}|$), using a mass-neutral perturbation to ignore the trivial mass mode.
Computing it is therefore not overhead — it is one of the results. As a bonus, a
multiplier crossing the unit circle would flag a bifurcation away from simple
$T$-periodicity (e.g. period doubling at extreme amplitude), so the method is
self-diagnosing.

**Harmonic balance (an efficient alternative, not yet implemented).** When the
response is smooth, one can avoid time stepping entirely by expanding
$P(\sigma, t) = \sum_n \hat P_n(\sigma)\,e^{i n\omega t}$ in a few Fourier
harmonics and solving directly for the amplitudes $\hat P_n$. This **harmonic
balance** method is typically the most efficient route for moderate LAOS; it
degrades only when the Lissajous response sharpens enough to need many harmonics.
It is noted here as natural future work; v0.1 uses the period-map solvers above.

### 13. Doing many runs at once: parallelism

Each individual solve above is cheap, but a real study sweeps a parameter: a flow
curve over many shear rates, a spectrum over many frequencies, a LAOS map over a
grid of amplitudes and frequencies. These points are completely independent of one
another — an "embarrassingly parallel" workload. The package therefore parallelises
at the **parameter level** (`sweep.py`): it farms the points out to a pool of
worker processes, each of which solves a few points by itself. The time stepping
*within* a single solve is left sequential, because it is causal — step $n+1$ needs
step $n$ — and cannot be parallelised across steps.

Two practical points make this scale cleanly. First, **no oversubscription**: the
underlying linear-algebra library (BLAS) will, by default, try to use all cores for
each matrix operation; if we *also* run one process per core, we would have cores
fighting each other. So each worker pins its BLAS to a single thread
(`threadpoolctl`), and the parallelism comes entirely from running many workers.
(The inner solves are small banded systems anyway, for which multi-threading is
pure overhead.) Second, **clean hand-off between processes**: separate processes do
not share memory, so the work functions live at module scope and receive only
simple, picklable arguments (the grid is passed as its defining numbers and rebuilt
inside the worker). With only one core available the pool degrades gracefully to
ordinary serial execution.

---

## Summary of the validation chain

The pieces check each other, which is the best defence against subtle errors:

- the discrete steady state reproduces the analytic identity
  $\alpha = \tfrac12 + \sqrt D + D$ to machine precision (operators + closure are
  right);
- the generator has exactly zero column sums and every run conserves mass to
  $\sim 10^{-8}$ (the scheme is genuinely conservative);
- the time-stepping **engine** relaxes to the same stress as the direct
  **oracle** to $\sim 10^{-11}$ (two independent methods agree);
- SAOS reproduces the Maxwell limits and the elastic plateau $G_0 = 1$;
- LAOS first-harmonic moduli converge to the SAOS moduli as $\gamma_0 \to 0$, with
  the residual vanishing as $\Delta t \to 0$ at the expected first-order rate, and
  even harmonics staying at the $10^{-8}$ noise floor;
- the critical exponents come out as $\Sigma \sim \dot\gamma^{1/5}$ and
  $\sigma_y \sim (\alpha_c - \alpha)^{1/2}$.
